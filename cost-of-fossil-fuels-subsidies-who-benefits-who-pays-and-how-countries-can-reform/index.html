The Real Cost of Fossil Fuels: Who Benefits, Who Pays, and How Countries Can Reform

Fossil fuels power much of modern life, from electricity and transportation to cooking, and heating. To keep energy affordable, governments spend billions subsidizing fossil fuels. Their environmental and social costs are far larger. Who benefits, why are subsidies hard to remove, and how have some countries designed successful reforms?

December 2026, Story by Samuel Kofi Tetteh-Baah & Jonas Helth Lønborg, Visuals by Maarten Lambrechts & Aendra Henriette Rininsland

As governments allocate public funds among competing priorities such as infrastructure, health, education, and defense, they must also respond to rising energy costs. Coal, gasoline, diesel, and natural gas have long powered electricity generation, transportation, industry, construction, cooking, and heating. Governments often subsidize fossil fuel consumption, which helps stabilize and lower energy prices for households and businesses. But these subsidies also impose significant economic and environmental costs and, once introduced, can be politically difficult to remove.
Fossil fuel subsidies can consume a substantial share of public budgets.
International Energy Agency. 2023. Fossil fuel subsidies database. Paris: IEA.
Black, S, Celniak, W, Garcia Huitron, A, Parry, I, Schulz Antipa, P and Vernon-Lin, N. 2025. Underpriced and Overused: Fossil Fuel Subsidies Data 2025 Update. International Monetary Fund.
Evaluating them is therefore essential to understanding their fiscal implications and whether public resources are being used effectively.
Globally, fossil fuel subsidies in 2023 amounted to one-fifth of total government expenditure on education.
Note: The estimate is based on World Bank staff calculations using the most recent data available. Government expenditure on education is calculated using GDP and government expenditure on education (% of GDP) from the October 2026 vintage of the World Development Indicators (WDI). Fossil fuel subsidies for 2024, obtained from IMF staff calculations (Black et al. 2025), are adjusted to 2023 prices using the U.S. GDP deflator.
Let’s explore how government spending on fossil fuel subsidies has evolved over time.
The fossil fuel subsidy estimates used in this story are from the International Monetary Fund (IMF), covering 170 countries, and measure explicit subsidies, primarily when fuel prices fall below supply costs. The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) uses a similar price-gap approach but also incorporates other forms of government support to fossil fuel producers and consumers. In 2024, UNEP estimated global subsidies at $921 billion, compared with the IMF's $725 billion. Despite differences in levels, both sources show similar trends, with subsidies peaking in 2022 before declining.
Beyond the global picture, the patterns look very different across countries at different income levels; particularly for net exporters of fossil fuels. Let’s explore how.
But what do these subsidies mean for the price consumers pay at the pump? In some countries, governments subsidize fuel consumption, keeping retail prices below the cost at which suppliers can provide it (supply costs). In others, governments impose taxes, pushing retail prices above supply costs. Subsidies generally encourage fuel consumption, while taxes discourage it. Let's explore how this works across countries.
Ensuring that energy is accessible and affordable is an important development goal. But they can also have unintended economic, social, and environmental consequences.

The unintended consequences of fossil fuel subsidies

Fossil fuel subsidies are often introduced to make energy affordable. But they carry significant unintended economic, social, and environmental costs.
Although fossil fuel subsidies are often intended to make energy affordable for poorer households, wealthier households tend to benefit more. This is because the latter generally consumes more fuel and therefore captures a larger share of the subsidies.
Subsidies also strain government budgets, create opportunities for corruption and cross-border smuggling, and slow the transition to cleaner energy.
Finally, by keeping prices artificially low, subsidies ‘distort’ markets and encourage people to consume more than they otherwise would. This increased consumption of fossil fuels can generate costs for society, including air pollution, increased global warming, road congestion, and accidents. These costs, which are not reflected in the price consumers pay, are known as ‘negative externalities’.

The full cost of fossil fuel use

The cost of these externalities of fossil fuel consumption are often not reflected in retail fuel prices.
Black, S, Celniak, W, Garcia Huitron, A, Parry, I, Schulz Antipa, P and Vernon-Lin, N. 2025. Underpriced and Overused: Fossil Fuel Subsidies Data 2025 Update. International Monetary Fund.
More efficient fuel pricing would cover the supply costs and these externalities. When retail prices fall below this level, governments not only subsidize fossil fuel consumption; they also forgo valuable tax revenue.
For example, gasoline may sell at the pump for just $0.30 per liter, even though its full cost to society is $1.25—including $0.50 in supply costs, $0.60 in environmental damages, and $0.15 in forgone VAT. The difference reflects costs not paid by consumers but borne by society through fiscal losses, environmental damages, and forgone tax revenue.
An International Monetary Fund study quantifies these costs by estimating the monetary value of carbon emissions, deaths from air pollution, and, for road fuels, congestion, accidents, and road damage.
Black, S, Celniak, W, Garcia Huitron, A, Parry, I, Schulz Antipa, P and Vernon-Lin, N. 2025. Underpriced and Overused: Fossil Fuel Subsidies Data 2025 Update. International Monetary Fund.
It then expresses these costs per unit of fuel and incorporates them into the efficient fuel price.
Typically, the retail price of fossil fuels falls short of the efficient price that would internalize all social and environmental costs. However, the gap between retail and efficient price differs by country income status and fuel type.
As shown above, the harmful impact of subsidizing fossil fuel use is not limited to the fiscal strain on government budgets. There are social and environmental costs to consider to understand the overall impact of fossil fuel use.

If subsidies are harmful, why are they so hard to remove?

Fossil fuel subsidies persist for a range of political, economic, and social reasons. For example, resource-rich countries may see affordable fuel as a way for citizens to share in the benefits of natural resource wealth, making reforms more challenging.
For households, the effects of subsidy reforms can be immediate and visible. Higher fuel prices can increase the cost of transportation, food, and other goods, placing additional pressure on household budgets and sometimes leading to public opposition.
Reforms can also be challenging because of the economic interests of industries and businesses that benefit from existing fuel pricing arrangements.
International Energy Agency. 2023. Fossil fuel subsidies database. Paris: IEA.

Fuel subsidy reforms

Because removing fossil fuel subsidies can be unpopular with the public, reforms can be politically challenging. But past reform experiences around the world offer valuable lessons on how to design and implement reforms to increase their chances of success and strengthen their sustainability.
Moerenhout, T, Gencer, D, Arizu, B, Lee, M A and Braun, H. 2024. Energy Subsidy Reform in Action: Political Economy Analysis and Communications for Energy Subsidy Reform: Approaches and Insights from Recent Technical Assistance. ESMAP Technical Report. Washington DC: World Bank.
Effective reform includes gradual subsidy reductions, protection for vulnerable households, clear communication, and visible improvements in public services. For example, to avoid sudden price increases, India raised diesel prices through small monthly increases beginning in 2013 before fully deregulating prices in 2014.
Kojima, M. 2016. Fossil Fuel Subsidy and Pricing Policies: Recent Developing Country Experience. Washington DC: World Bank.
Targeted support can help households cope with higher prices. For example, Indonesia provided cash transfers to poor and near-poor households alongside its 2005 fuel subsidy reform.
World Bank. 2006. Making the New Indonesia Work for the Poor. Washington DC: World Bank.
Clear communication is also important. For example, Morocco supported its 2012 energy subsidy reform with a television, radio, and print campaign explaining the fiscal costs of subsidies and their unequal benefits.
Fritz, V, Levy, B and Ort, R (eds.). 2014. Problem-Driven Political Economy Analysis: The World Bank's Experience. Washington DC: World Bank.
Moerenhout, T, Gencer, D, Arizu, B, Lee, M A and Braun, H. 2024. Energy Subsidy Reform in Action: Political Economy Analysis and Communications for Energy Subsidy Reform: Approaches and Insights from Recent Technical Assistance. ESMAP Technical Report. Washington DC: World Bank.
Finally, using subsidy savings to improve services that people value can make the benefits of reform tangible. For example, Ghana used savings from its 2005 fuel subsidy reform to expand education, health care, public transport, and rural electrification.
Hoy, C, Kim, Y S, Nguyen, M C, Sosa, M and Tiwari, S. 2026. “Attitudes Towards Reducing Fossil Fuel Subsidies: Evidence Across 12 Middle-income Countries.” Journal of Development Economics 178: 103612.
Inchauste, G and Victor, D G (eds.) 2017. The Political Economy of Energy Subsidy Reform. Washington DC: World Bank.
The World Bank has supported countries such as Indonesia and Morocco in designing and implementing energy subsidy reforms through policy advice, technical assistance, and measures to protect vulnerable households. However, ownership and responsibility for these reforms ultimately rest with national governments.

The case for fossil fuel subsidy reform

The consumption of fossil fuels drives climate change and local air pollution, yet governments continue to spend heavily on subsidies. Between 2015 and 2024, fossil fuel subsidies increased by more than $100 billion to $725 billion.
Black, S, Celniak, W, Garcia Huitron, A, Parry, I, Schulz Antipa, P and Vernon-Lin, N. 2025. Underpriced and Overused: Fossil Fuel Subsidies Data 2025 Update. International Monetary Fund.
In more than half of countries, fossil fuel subsidies per capita have increased over the past decade. While some countries have reduced subsidies, reforms have sometimes triggered public protests. Yet evidence from middle-income countries suggests that public support for subsidy removal could be stronger if the resources saved were redirected toward productive investments, including infrastructure, education, and healthcare.
Hoy, C, Kim, Y S, Nguyen, M C, Sosa, M and Tiwari, S. 2026. “Attitudes Towards Reducing Fossil Fuel Subsidies: Evidence Across 12 Middle-income Countries.” Journal of Development Economics 178: 103612.
Fully reforming fossil fuel prices to reflect supply and environmental costs could cut global carbon dioxide emissions by about 46 percent below baseline levels by 2035, increase government revenues by roughly 3.3 percent of global GDP, and prevent around 1.1 million premature deaths each year from local air pollution.
Black, S, Celniak, W, Garcia Huitron, A, Parry, I, Schulz Antipa, P and Vernon-Lin, N. 2025. Underpriced and Overused: Fossil Fuel Subsidies Data 2025 Update. International Monetary Fund.
