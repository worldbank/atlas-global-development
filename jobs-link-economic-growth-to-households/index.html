Growth and Jobs

Jobs Link Economic Growth to Households

Economic growth has lifted nearly 1.5 billion people out of extreme poverty since 1990, but it does not automatically bring prosperity for all. Jobs connect a growing economy to household living standards, and investment can speed up growth and open the way to better jobs. So how have economies turned growth into more and better jobs?

December 2026, Story by Nishant Yonzan, Visuals by Maarten Lambrechts

Growth has been good for the poor

Income growth has been and continues to be good for the poor,
Dollar, D and Kraay, A. 2002. “Growth is Good for the Poor.” Journal of economic growth 7(3): 195-225.
Dollar, D, Kleineberg, T, and Kraay, A. 2016. Growth still is good for the poor. European Economic Review: 81, 68-85.
particularly when gains are shared evenly.
Lønborg, J H, Lambrechts, M & Yonzan, N. 2026. Combining income growth with inequality reduction benefits everyone. In Atlas of Global Development 2026, edited by A F Pirlea, D Wadhwa, D Mahler, U Serajuddin, M Welch, A Thudt and M Lambrechts. Washington DC: World Bank.
World Bank. 2026. Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet Report: Jobs at the Center of Poverty Eradication. World Bank Group, Washington DC.
More and better jobs are one way the gains from growth reach households, through higher earnings and greater financial security. But growth does not automatically deliver better jobs. Sound policies can help firms invest, expand, and hire, while giving more people the skills and opportunities to benefit.
Let’s start with how growth has changed poverty.
Over recent decades, economic growth has transformed living standards across much of the world. One of the most visible impacts has been the fall in global extreme poverty—that is, those living on less than $3 per day.
Values reported in this chapter are expressed in 2021 prices adjusted for differences in living costs across countries (purchasing power parity).
Due to sustained economic growth, average income per person has almost doubled in the past 35 years, lifting more than 1.5 billion people out of extreme poverty.
International Comparison Program (ICP), World Bank. Downloaded from World Development Indicators (NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.KD). 2026. Washington DC: World Bank. (accessed October 9, 2026).
World Bank. 2026. Poverty and Inequality Platform (version 20260922_2021_01_02_PROD), World Bank Group.
For ease of reference, “average income per capita” is used instead of “GDP per capita” in the text while GDP per capita is used in the visualizations.
Economic growth has been instrumental for moving people out of poverty. To see how, let’s consider the following thought experiment.

1 in 3 people

worldwide would be living in extreme poverty today, rather than 1 in 10, had there been no income growth over the last 35 years.

Diverging fortunes across countries

Income growth has brought remarkable progress, but the global picture hides some important differences. Although high- and middle-income economies have generally weathered major shocks, many low-income and fragile and conflict-affected economies have fallen further behind. Growth in these cases is still far too slow to make a meaningful dent in poverty.
Economies need growth. But in many cases, those that need growth the most have seen their incomes stall or fall since the pandemic, with conflict and overlapping food, fertilizer, and energy shocks widening disparity and slowing poverty reduction.
World Bank. 2026. Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet Report: Jobs at the Center of Poverty Eradication. World Bank Group, Washington DC.
Artuc, E, Do, Q-T, Galasso, E, Ghose, D, Lakner, C, Mattoo, A, Rijkers, B, Rozo, S, Tortarolo, D and Yonzan, N. 2026. Responding to the Global Shock from the War in the Middle East. Washington DC, World Bank Group.
The map below highlights these cases.
Poverty is becoming more concentrated in fewer countries, especially those affected by fragility and conflict. The World Bank’s 2026 Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet Report unpacks why this is happening.
World Bank. 2026. Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet Report: Jobs at the Center of Poverty Eradication. World Bank Group, Washington DC.
In these economies, education and urbanization—usually linked with falling poverty—have not brought the same progress.
Nguyen, M C, Yonzan, N, Brunckhorst, B J, Kwizera, S, Lakner, C, Lara Ibarra, G and Stemmler, H. 2026. Education and Urbanization Alone May Not End Global Extreme Poverty. World Bank blogs, October 28.
Nguyen, M C, Yonzan, N, Brunckhorst, B J, Kwizera, S, Lakner, C, Lara Ibarra, G and Stemmler, H. 2026. Beyond the Headcount: The Changing Geography and Composition of Global Poverty, 2003–2024. Washington, DC: World Bank Group.
To reduce poverty and increase livelihoods of the poor, these countries need faster income growth, and to share any gains more widely.

Viet Nam shows how growth can transform living standards beyond poverty

Growth is about more than escaping poverty. It can also open the door to better livelihoods and a better quality of life. Viet Nam shows what this can look like.
In the mid-1980s, Viet Nam was a poor, largely agricultural economy. The Đổi Mới reforms, launched in 1986, helped change the country’s path, moving it from central planning toward a market economy. Reforms included replacing collective farming with a system that allows farmers to make their own production and marketing decisions, liberalizing agricultural prices, unifying the exchange rate, and relaxing restrictions on foreign direct investment and private business. Closer links with the global economy, foreign investment, macroeconomic stability, and investment in people helped sustain rapid growth and improve livelihoods over many years. This helped incomes grow more than fivefold from about $2,500 in 1990 to $15,500 by 2025.
World Bank. World Development Indicators (NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.KD). 2026. Washington DC: World Bank. (accessed November 20, 2026).
And as the economy grew, people’s working lives changed too. Workers gradually moved from lower-productivity, informal agricultural work into manufacturing and services. New factories and businesses needed more workers, productivity increased, and household incomes rose. Between 1990 and 2025, these shifts helped create not just more jobs, but more stable and more formal jobs.

The share of employment in wage and salaried jobs was

2.5 times

higher in 2025, compared with 1990.

Viet Nam shows how a growing economy can improve everyday life. As businesses expanded and the economy changed, new job types emerged, and higher earnings brought the benefits of growth into people’s homes. Such gains do not automatically reach everyone. But when growth is sustained and policies help more people find jobs and move into more productive work, it can bring large, lasting improvements in living standards.

The world will need more jobs

For households, growth becomes real when it brings a job, better pay, or more secure work. Jobs are the bridge between a growing economy and people’s everyday living standards. More jobs matter because growing working-age populations need opportunities to work. Initial projections suggest that 1.2 billion young people mostly in low and middle income countries will reach working age over the next decade. This is expected to be the largest cohort looking for jobs. Of these, just over 400 million are expected to find jobs; around 500 million are likely to be continuing their education; and some 300 million may be out of school and not employed.
But it is not all about the number of jobs. Quality of jobs also matters. Even a growing economy that creates jobs can leave workers struggling if those jobs are poorly paid, insecure, or offer little chance to build skills. Sound policies, like those adopted by Viet Nam, help turn growth into better work and higher household incomes.
Hovhannisyan, S, Montalva, V, Stamm, K, Rodriguez Catelan, C. 2024. New Insights for Assessing and Improving Job Quality in Developing Countries. Let’s Talk Development. World Bank blogs, December 11.
They can also widen access to these opportunities, helping more people share in prosperity rather than concentrating gains among a few.

Jobs are difficult to come by in poorer countries

Growth does not deliver the same job gains everywhere. In some countries, it brings more jobs and better pay. In others, wages stay low, work remains insecure, and many young people find themselves outside work, school, or training. In richer economies, employment responds more strongly to growth. Over the medium term, an increase in economic output leads to much higher increase in employment in a rich economy compared with their non-rich counterparts.
Chrimes, T, Kose, M A and Stamm, K (eds). 2026. The Global Jobs Challenge. Washington DC: World Bank.
The latter therefore need faster growth, alongside sound policies that help firms create jobs and workers access them. Growth alone is not a strategy for better jobs or shared prosperity.
This weak link between growth and jobs matters most for poor households. Income through jobs is the main and often only asset of the poor, yet their work yields less income per household member than the work of the nonpoor. The gap is not mainly about finding any job: working-age adults in poor households are only slightly less likely to have a job than those in nonpoor households, with employment rates just six percentage points lower. But poor workers put in three to four fewer hours a week and support three times as many dependents as workers in the richest households.
World Bank. 2026. Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet Report: Jobs at the Center of Poverty Eradication. World Bank Group, Washington DC.
Without faster growth and policies that help firms create jobs and workers access them, poor households are likely to face a large jobs deficit.

Workers in poor economies support

3 times

as many dependents as their rich country counterparts.

Investment can support faster income growth and better jobs

As economies develop, workers can move from less productive into more productive jobs in agriculture, manufacturing, and services. This is a win-win situation, as businesses produce more and workers earn more.
This shift requires public and private investment in physical, digital, and human capital. Investment enables businesses to grow, adopt better technology, enter new markets, and hire more workers. A recent World Bank blog finds that during 115 periods of rapid investment growth in 59 low- and middle-income economies over more than 50 years, annual investment growth rose from about 3 percent to more than 10 percent.
Gill, I and Kose, A. 2025. Sparking the Investment Miracle Developing Economies Need to Create Jobs. Voices. World Bank blogs, September 19.
Income growth per person also doubled, and productivity growth tripled. Workers moved more quickly from agriculture into manufacturing and services. Exports and foreign investment increased, while poverty fell rapidly. Together, these changes transformed the economies.
Foreign direct investment can be an important source of finance that supports growth, especially in poorer countries. It played a major role in Viet Nam’s development, where net foreign direct investment reached $18 billion (4.2 percent of the economy) in 2025, up from just over $1 billion in 1990. This form of investment can also bring new technology, connect local firms to supply chains and export markets, and create better jobs.
Foreign investment makes the biggest difference when it connects with the local economy. Projects that hire and train local workers, buy from local suppliers, and strengthen domestic firms can boost jobs, incomes, and productivity. Benefits do not happen automatically; rather they are context-specific.
Jordaan, J A, Douw, W and Qiang, Z. 2020. Multinational Corporation Affiliates, Backward Linkages, and Productivity Spillovers in Developing and Emerging Economies : Evidence and Policy Making. Washington DC: World Bank Group.

A jobs strategy catered to each country

Growth has helped reduce poverty, but that does not mean it automatically leads to shared prosperity. The goal is for growth to benefit all households. When accompanied by policies that remove barriers which keep firms from investing, expanding, and hiring, growth can create more numerous, better-paid and more secure jobs, bringing growth into people’s homes.
No single approach works everywhere. Each country needs a job strategy that fits its circumstances, built around three connected pillars: First, building the infrastructure and skills that workers and businesses need, including transport and care services that make jobs more accessible, especially for women and young people. Second, creating an environment where firms can grow, through stable policies, clear rules, fair competition and trade, and affordable finance. Third, attracting private investment and helping foreign investors work with local suppliers, including small and women-led firms.
Worker protections and social protection can help more people, including disadvantaged groups, benefit from safer, more secure, and better-paid work.
World Bank. 2024. Global Economic Prospects, January 2024. Washington DC: World Bank.
Together, these policy pillars can strengthen the link between economic growth and employment, so that a growing economy means a better living for more people.
Chrimes, T, Kose, M A and Stamm, K (eds). 2026. The Global Jobs Challenge. Washington DC: World Bank.
