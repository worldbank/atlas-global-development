Why Legal Identification Matters For Global Development

Over the past decade, the world has made steady progress toward universal ID coverage. Yet hundreds of millions of people still have no way to prove who they are. Most of these are children and the poor, and the gap is widest in Sub-Saharan Africa. What will it take to close the global ID gap?

December 2026, Story by Ana Florina Pirlea & Jorin Margaux Wolff, Visuals by Maarten Lambrechts, Illustrations: Drew Bardana

An official form of ID provides access to social programs, health care, education, and legal protections. It also allows people to open a bank account or get a SIM card, work in the formal sector, run a business, and own land and other assets. Yet, millions of people, particularly those in lower-income economies and marginalized or vulnerable groups, still lack an official government-issued ID that confirms their legal identity.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.

Who lacks official proof of identity?

Over the past decade, the world has made steady progress on this front. Between 2017 and 2025, the number of people without proof of legal identity fell from around 1 billion to 805 million, even as the world’s population grew. Coverage is now 95 percent among adults, with the remaining gap increasingly concentrated among children. More than half of those without ID are children whose births were never registered.
The global identification gap is also heavily concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for more than half of the world’s unregistered population. The number of children in this region without birth registration exceeds the number of adults without foundational ID, showing how exclusion can begin at birth and persist later in life. The balance flips in regions like East Asia and Pacific or Latin America and the Caribbean, where adults account for the larger share of the identification gap.
The cutoff age for splitting populations into children and adults is determined by the adult metric used when collecting the data. For Findex data, the cutoff is either the minimum legal age for obtaining the foundational ID or age 15, whichever is higher. For voter registration data, the cutoff is the voting age, typically 18. For administrative data, the cutoff is either ID eligibility age, or age 5 in economies that allow registration from birth. The under-5 birth registration rate was then applied to the entire child population below the cutoff age. There are no cross-nationally comparable registration data for the 5–15 age range, so the under-5 rate serves as a proxy for all children below ID age. The rate is an under-5 rate, but the population it is applied to is roughly 0–14 in most economies.
Clark, J.M., Metz, A.Z., & Casher, C.S. (2022). ID4D Global Database 2021: Global ID Coverage Estimates. Washington, DC: World Bank.
Reported gaps in ID ownership may also include people whose credentials have been lost or expired, and those who do not perceive a need to own an ID.

Children without birth registration

Birth registration is usually a child’s first recognition in the eyes of the state, providing the legal identity needed to access rights and opportunities throughout life.
UNICEF. (2024). The right start in life: Global levels and trends in birth registration 2024 update. New York, NY: United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF).
Without it, children can be excluded from education, health care, and social protection, increasing their vulnerability to poverty and social exclusion.
UNICEF. (2024). The right start in life: Global levels and trends in birth registration 2024 update. New York, NY: United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF).
Kusumaputra, I. M., Mahardika, M. C., Hayati, B. N., & Itu, Y. (2025). Exclusion from birth: Structural barriers and social impacts absence of birth certificates for children of urban vulnerable groups. The Journal of Philosophical Economics: Reflections on Economic and Social Issues (in press). HAL Archive.
Lack of birth registration also heightens the risk of child labor, trafficking, child marriage, and other forms of exploitation, as well as statelessness, particularly among marginalized, migrant, and refugee populations.
European Network on Statelessness (ENS). (2024). Birth registration and the prevention of statelessness in Europe: Identifying good practices and remaining barriers (Thematic briefing, updated November 2024). European Network on Statelessness.
UNICEF. (2024). The right start in life: Global levels and trends in birth registration 2024 update. New York, NY: United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF).
The lack of birth registration can also make it much more difficult for people to obtain a formal, government-issued ID as adults.
For children under the age of ID eligibility, proof of legal identity is provided through birth registration.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.
The under-5 birth registration rate is the only internationally comparable measure of birth registration. This is near universal across Europe, North America, and much of Latin America, East Asia & the Pacific, but falls sharply in South Asia (76 percent) and Sub-Saharan Africa, where only 52 percent of young children are registered. In a handful of countries, including Ethiopia, Somalia and Zambia, fewer than 1 child in 10 are registered.
UNICEF. (2024). The right start in life: Global levels and trends in birth registration 2024 update. New York, NY: United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF).
Birth registration records a child’s existence in the civil registry, while a birth certificate provides documentary proof of that registration. It is therefore possible for a child to be registered but lack a birth certificate.
However, the gap does not close on a child’s fifth birthday. A child who is not registered often stays unregistered until they are old enough to apply for an adult ID, usually between the ages of 15 and 18. Furthermore it is often very difficult for people whose birth has not been registered to apply for an ID.
Globally, approximately 458 million children, who are below the ID eligibility age of their country, are missing birth registration, making them legally invisible for their governments.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.

458 million

The effects extend beyond individuals. Incomplete birth registration means governments have an incomplete picture of their populations, making it harder to plan services, direct resources, and identify communities that are being left behind.
UNICEF. (2024). The right start in life: Global levels and trends in birth registration 2024 update. New York, NY: United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF).
Kusumaputra, I. M., Mahardika, M. C., Hayati, B. N., & Itu, Y. (2025). Exclusion from birth: Structural barriers and social impacts absence of birth certificates for children of urban vulnerable groups. The Journal of Philosophical Economics: Reflections on Economic and Social Issues (in press). HAL Archive.

Adults without foundational ID

Most regions have achieved near universal coverage when it comes to adult ID. Coverage is lowest in Sub Saharan Africa, but the region is making fast progress.
For adults, proof of legal identity is usually provided through foundational ID systems, such as: national ID, population registries, and civil registration. In some contexts it is provided through functional ID issued for specific purposes, such as a passport, drivers’ license, or voter ID.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.
In recent years several Sub-Saharan African and South Asian countries have made rapid progress in expanding the availability of modern forms of identification for the general population.
Through initiatives like the Fayda ID program in Ethiopia, and the West Africa Unique Identification for Regional Integration and Inclusion (WURI) Project in Benin, Burkina Faso, Togo and Niger – both supported by the World Bank – millions of people have obtained a government-recognized digital ID that facilitates access to services.
Data are lacking for several lower income economies that are facing ongoing or recent conflict, such as Yemen or Syria, but ID coverage is known to be low.

Structural barriers to obtaining identification

For millions of people, getting an official ID involves more than completing a registration form. Long distances to registration centers, missing supporting documents, and direct or indirect costs are common barriers that prevent people from obtaining an ID, particularly if they already face social and economic disadvantages.
Missing documentation can create a cycle of exclusion. A birth certificate may be required to obtain a foundational ID; yet for those whose births were never registered, there may be no straightforward way to obtain one. Lacking one document can prevent people from obtaining another.
These barriers fall hardest on vulnerable groups: in low-income economies with ID coverage below 90 percent, adults with primary education or less are nearly 12 percentage points less likely to have an ID. People who are out of the workforce, live in rural areas, or have a lower income, and women are also disadvantaged, and when these factors overlap, as they often do, the risk of exclusion is compounded.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.

Limited access to essential services

Without an official ID, routine activities such as obtaining mobile service, opening a bank account, applying for a job, or receiving public support can become difficult or impossible. In approximately 160 economies, proof of identity is required to register a SIM card for a cell phone or other mobile device.
"GSMA. 2025. “Mandatory registration of prepaid SIMs” Mobile Policy Handbook. Retrieved March 10, 2026
In Uganda almost 80 percent of the adults without an ID reported being unable to obtain mobile service, greatly limiting their access to connectivity and an expanding range of digital services, as well as their ability to benefit from the digital economy.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.
The lack of an official ID can also greatly compromise other aspects of a person’s role in society. In Mozambique, more than 40 percent of adults without official ID report difficulty participating in elections, illustrating how gaps in identification can also restrict civic participation.

Digital identification

Official IDs are often required for accessing social assistance, healthcare, financial services, formal employment, and other services.
Clark, J.M., Metz, A.Z., & Casher, C.S. (2022). ID4D Global Database 2021: Global ID Coverage Estimates. Washington, DC: World Bank.
Digital IDs extend the benefits of ID to the digital world by enabling people to prove and authenticate their identity anytime, anywhere. They allow people to prove who they are remotely and securely, and then reuse their trusted digital identity across different public and private services, without having to verify their identity each time. As more transactions move online, economies are increasingly developing digital ID ecosystems as part of their digital public infrastructure.
World Bank. 2022. Federated Ecosystems for Digital ID: Current Approaches and Lessons. Washington, DC: World Bank Group.
Access to digital identification remains highly unequal: an estimated 2.8 billion people live in economies (mostly low- and lower-middle-income) without a digital ID system that is capable of secure remote authentication.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.
Digital identification can take different forms, and these build on each other. At the most basic level, identity records are stored electronically rather than on paper. At the next level, identity information can be checked digitally during in-person transactions. At the most advanced level, people can securely prove who they are remotely, to access services and transact online.
Metz, A., Casher, C., and Clark, J. (2024). ID4D Global Dataset Volume 2: Digital Identification Progress and Gaps. Washington, DC: World Bank.
The World Bank counts an economy as having a digital ID system when it is at the most advanced level. For this, it needs to pass three tests. First, the system is securely linked to the real person who holds it. Second, it is widely used by the general public rather than only in pilots. And third, it can be used across multiple online services. By this measure, only 89 economies had digital ID systems in 2024.
Availability alone is not enough. Even where access to digital ID systems exists, many people remain unenrolled, and use often lags ownership.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.
Meaningful use depends on many things. This includes whether digital IDs are accepted across public and private services, whether people have the technology and skills needed to use them, how user-friendly the technology is, and whether legal and governance frameworks protect users’ rights. Above all, people need to see a clear benefit in using their credentials.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.

Ethiopia: from fragmented paper records to a national digital ID

Until recently, Ethiopia lacked a national foundational ID system with digital verification. In practice, people relied on locally issued paper IDs, which varied widely and were difficult for service providers to authenticate. In 2017, more than 35 percent of adults lacked even this form of identification, with substantially wider gaps among women.
World Bank. 2023. Ethiopia Digital ID for Inclusion and Services Project: Project Appraisal Document. Washington, DC: World Bank.
Ethiopia is now rolling out Fayda ID, a unified national digital ID designed to give residents secure, government-recognized proof of identity. The World Bank-supported “Digital ID for Inclusion and Services Project” is helping expand registration, strengthen data-protection institutions, and integrate the Fayda ID into public and private services.
World Bank. 2026. Ethiopia Digital ID for Inclusion and Services Project (P179040): Implementation Support Review, March 23–30, 2026: Aide Memoire. Washington, DC: World Bank.
By October 2026, more than 54 million people in Ethiopia (or approximately 40 percent of the total population) had been registered in the Fayda digital ID program.
National ID Program (NIDP), Ethiopia. 2026. Fayda: Ethiopia’s Digital ID.
Early process testing illustrates the potential benefits. In controlled comparisons, Fayda-enabled verification reduced the estimated time and cost of opening a bank account and obtaining a SIM card. These findings are projections rather than measured system-wide outcomes, but they show how trusted digital verification could make routine service provision faster and more accessible.
Ethiopian National ID Program. n.d. Fayda Integration: Evidence of Faster, Smarter Service Delivery. Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: Ethiopian National ID Program.

How ID4D is helping countries close the identification gap

The World Bank’s Identification for Development (ID4D) Initiative helps countries build inclusive, trusted, and effective identification systems so that everyone can prove who they are and access services, rights, and economic opportunities. ID4D currently works with more than 80 countries to make identification more accessible, secure, and useful.
ID4D supports everything from birth registration and national ID systems to digital identity tools for online and in-person services. It also helps countries strengthen the laws, safeguards, and technology behind these systems and shares research and lessons on what does and does not work.
World Bank. 2025. Annual Report 2024–25: 10 Years of Impact. Putting People at the Center of Digital Transformation. Washington, DC: World Bank Group.
Closing the identification gap requires going beyond issuing credentials to ensuring that identity systems are inclusive, trusted, and useful. Priorities include: strengthening civil registration from birth; removing cost, distance, and documentation requirement barriers; designing systems for underserved populations; and ensuring ID systems enable access to essential services and economic opportunities.
Digital ID systems must also be accessible to people with limited access to technology or digital skills, and widely accepted across public and private services. Ultimately, success should be measured by whether everyone can reliably prove their identity, in person or online, in ways that allow them to access services, expand opportunities, and protect their rights.
Ansar, S.; Clark, J.M.; Wolff, J.M.. (2026). ID4D Global Dataset 2025: Trends in Identification for Development. © World Bank.
