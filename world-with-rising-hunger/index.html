Reversing Gains: A World With Rising Hunger

After decades of progress, global hunger has been rising again since 2015, hitting Sub Saharan Africa and the Middle East the hardest. Escalating conflicts, frequent climate shocks, and fragile food supply chains have fueled this reversal. Are countries still on track to end hunger, and which ones are beating the odds?

December 2026, Story by Divyanshi Wadhwa, Visuals by Maarten Lambrechts

Global rise in hunger

Access to enough food is a basic human necessity.
FAO. Right to Food. Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations.
Yet this necessity remains unmet for over 650 million people globally.
So how do we measure hunger? One widely used measure is prevalence of undernourishment: the share of people whose usual food intake does not provide enough energy to maintain a normal, active, healthy life.
FAO. “Indicator 2.1.1 - Prevalence of undernourishment.” SDG Indicators Data Portal. Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations.
And while the world made steady progress against hunger for decades, that progress has stalled and, in many places, reversed. Over the past decade, global hunger has risen at an alarming rate.

In 2024

650  million people

were undernourished.

Are countries on track to end hunger?

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) set an ambitious target to end hunger by 2030 (SDG 2.1). A country is considered to have achieved this target when its prevalence of undernourishment falls to 2.5 percent or below.
FAO. Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), Retrieved from World Development Indicators (SN.ITK.DEFC.ZS).
But with global progress reversing in recent years, the world is increasingly off track. So how are individual countries performing against this target and where is meaningful progress being made?
What drove Senegal's rapid progress against malnutrition? The answer lies in a combination of sustained economic growth, agricultural gains, and deliberate policy choices that reinforced each other over time.
At the foundation, stable economic growth helped reduce poverty, while cereal yields improved at a rate faster than the Sub-Saharan African average, expanding the overall food availability. These structural gains created the conditions for better nutrition outcomes.
Samanpreet Brar, Nadia Akseer, Mohamadou Sall, Kaitlin Conway, Ibrahima Diouf, Karl Everett, Muhammad Islam, Papa Ibrahima Sylmang Sène, Hana Tasic, Jannah Wigle, Zulfiqar Bhutta, Drivers of stunting reduction in Senegal: a country case study, The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, Volume 112, Supplement 2, 2020, Pages 860S-874S, ISSN 0002-9165,
But these gains were not enough on their own.
The government established the Cellule de Lutte contre la Malnutrition (CLM), housed in the Prime Minister's Office, as a multisectoral coordination body bringing together health, education, water, sanitation, and agriculture ministries under a single governance framework. This approach is widely credited as central to Senegal’s success in reducing child stunting – a condition in which children are too short for their age because of chronic malnutrition, which can impair physical and cognitive development and have lasting effects on health, learning, and future earning potential. It made nutrition a whole-of-government priority, recognizing that progress depends on coordinated action across multiple sectors, not just the health system.
Samanpreet Brar, Nadia Akseer, Mohamadou Sall, Kaitlin Conway, Ibrahima Diouf, Karl Everett, Muhammad Islam, Papa Ibrahima Sylmang Sène, Hana Tasic, Jannah Wigle, Zulfiqar Bhutta, Drivers of stunting reduction in Senegal: a country case study, The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, Volume 112, Supplement 2, 2020, Pages 860S-874S, ISSN 0002-9165,
Operating within this framework, large-scale programs delivered growth monitoring, supplementary feeding, caregiver education, malnutrition screening, and early-childhood services at community level.
Samanpreet Brar, Nadia Akseer, Mohamadou Sall, Kaitlin Conway, Ibrahima Diouf, Karl Everett, Muhammad Islam, Papa Ibrahima Sylmang Sène, Hana Tasic, Jannah Wigle, Zulfiqar Bhutta, Drivers of stunting reduction in Senegal: a country case study, The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, Volume 112, Supplement 2, 2020, Pages 860S-874S, ISSN 0002-9165,
Although Senegal's story is one of meaningful, coordinated progress, it is not yet a complete one. Dietary diversity remains insufficient in many areas, breastfeeding practices fall short of recommended standards, and access to services is still limited in the most food-insecure regions.
Measuring hunger is challenging, and the prevalence of undernourishment captures only part of the picture. This measure does not rely on household survey data, a common source of development statistics. Instead, it draws on national accounts, combining national-level information on food production and availability, income levels, and population to produce a rough estimate of inadequate calorie intake.
FAO. Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), Retrieved from World Development Indicators (SN.ITK.DEFC.ZS).
This approach has clear limitations as it cannot capture individual or household experiences of food insecurity. Yet, it remains broadly comparable across countries and time, and as a result, provides the most consistent long-term global measure of hunger.
FAO. “Indicator 2.1.1 - Prevalence of undernourishment.” SDG Indicators Data Portal. Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations.

Where are the world's hunger hotspots?

To complement this long-term view, measuring hunger hotspots captures severe and often sudden crises. The Hunger Hotspots Report—a collaborative early-warning product launched in 2020 and updated every three to four months— identifies where conditions are most likely to deteriorate. The report flags countries and economies at highest risk of acute food insecurity in the near term and highlights the key drivers pushing people toward hunger.
WFP and FAO. 2026. Hunger Hotspots. FAO–WFP Early Warnings on Acute Food Insecurity: June to November 2026 Outlook. Rome: World Food Program and the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations.
It identifies hunger hotspots by combining current and projected acute food insecurity data with analysis of likely future shocks—including conflict, economic instability, and extreme weather—as well as underlying vulnerabilities and humanitarian access constraints. It then complements these data with expert assessments, ultimately identifying those requiring urgent action.
Looking at today's hunger hotspots and their underlying drivers helps explain the state of hunger and why it is rising.

What is driving the rise in hunger?

The primary drivers of food insecurity in these countries and economies are often linked to conflict (for example, insecurity, displacement, or social instability) and climate (for example, droughts, flooding, or tropical cyclones). The economic impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic also left many countries and economies struggling to recover from significant job losses.
World Bank. 2022. World Development Report 2022: Finance for an Equitable Recovery.
Rising global food prices have hit hardest in areas that rely on imports due to their lack of local food production.
UNCTAD. No date. Trade Against Hunger: Exploring Trade Actions to Fight Acute Food Insecurity and the Threat of Famine. United Nations Trade and Development.
First, let’s examine how conflict drives hunger.

Conflict

Armed conflict is one of the leading drivers of hunger worldwide, with impacts that extend far beyond the battlefield. The prevalence of undernourishment has been consistently higher in fragile and conflict-affected economies than in more stable economies.
FAO. Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), Retrieved from World Development Indicators (SN.ITK.DEFC.ZS).
In fragile and conflict-affected economies, about 1 in 5 people face hunger.
Conflict destabilizes entire food systems, ultimately triggering widespread food insecurity that can engulf whole countries and economies and spill over into neighboring regions.
Omamo, S W. 2025. “How Conflict Drives Hunger: Six Channels Through the Food System.” IFPRI blog, January 24.
Food systems are vulnerable to disruption at every stage, including food production,
Conflict often destroys farmland, livestock, and irrigation systems, while displacement forces families to abandon their crops and livelihoods.
food supply,
Local markets and supply chains break down as roads are blocked, and food becomes hard to find. With low availability in the markets, food prices rise sharply, making even the basics unaffordable and forcing many people to rely on humanitarian assistance.
economic shocks,
Food insecurity can worsen even without food supply disruptions. Economic instability—driven by contracting economies, high inflation, or currency devaluation—can push food prices upward, making it harder for households to afford food.
and aid delivery.
Omamo, S W. 2025. “How Conflict Drives Hunger: Six Channels Through the Food System.” IFPRI blog, January 24.
Such disruptions lead to rising food prices, and while price spikes often begin in areas that are directly affected by violence, their effects tend to ripple across entire economies because food supply chains are deeply interconnected. Myanmar offers a stark example.
Are you curious about how food prices have changed over time for other countries? Explore the World Bank Group’s dashboard to track monthly food price inflation estimates by country.
Some conflicts have impacts that reach beyond national and regional borders. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, for instance, disrupted global agricultural exports, including wheat, maize, fertilizer, and other staples. With both countries among the world’s major grain exporters, the sudden interruption in supply chains sent global food prices soaring, hitting low-income and import-dependent countries hardest. The crisis underscored the vulnerability of global food systems to large-scale conflicts, as prices remained volatile and supplies constrained until alternative trade routes and new supply channels were gradually established.
European Council. “How the Russian Invasion of Ukraine Has Further Aggravated the Global Food Crisis.” Archived content.
Conflict frequently also traps communities in a cycle of poverty and food insecurity, as families lose their livelihoods and assets, leaving them unable to recover after the fighting stops. As a result, addressing hunger in conflict-affected regions is a humanitarian priority.
Next, let’s look at how climate impacts hunger.

Climate

Food security depends on productive agriculture that can keep up with the growing demand of a rising global population. And farming depends on stable climate conditions, and is highly sensitive to shifts in temperature, rainfall, and other weather patterns.
FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP and WHO. 2018. The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2018. Building climate resilience for food security and nutrition. Rome, FAO.
With rising global temperatures and an increase in climate-related natural hazard events, agricultural productivity growth is expected to slow down
Ortiz-Bobea, A, Ault, T R, Carrillo, C M, Chambers, R G and Lobell, D B. 2021. “Anthropogenic Climate Change Has Slowed Global Agricultural Productivity Growth.” Nature Climate Change 11: 306–312.
with severe consequences for food security.
Thomas, V and Lopez, R. 2015. Global Increase in Climate-Related Disasters. Asian Development Bank Economics Working Paper Series No. 466.
Climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of droughts, floods, storms, and extreme heat with severe consequences for food security.
This point is illustrated by the devastating floods that struck Pakistan’s Sindh province in August 2022, among the worst in the country’s history. To understand how the floods affected agriculture, we use satellite images to measure the health of the province’s vegetation before and after the disaster. Using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) helps us compare how green the land was before and after the floods in order to estimate the extent of crop damage and how different areas were affected.
Qamer, F M, Abbas, S, Ahmad, B, Hussain, A, Salman, A, Muhammad, D, Nawaz, M, Shrestha, S, Iqbal, B and Thapa, S. 2023. “A Framework for Multi-sensor Satellite Data to Evaluate Crop Production Losses: The Case Study of 2022 Pakistan Floods.” Nature Scientific Reports 13: 4240.
In such natural hazard events, disruptions are not confined to the destruction of agricultural land. Storage and food processing infrastructure can also get damaged and transport routes blocked, affecting the entire food supply chain. For communities already facing poverty or marginalization, such shocks translate into immediate food insecurity, as households lose both subsistence harvests and market access.
WFP USA. 2024. “How Climate Change is Causing World Hunger.” World Food Program USA, April 29 (updated February 3, 2026).
This risk is especially pressing today. Forecasts point to a strong El Niño, a natural climate pattern that warms ocean waters in the Pacific and can disrupt weather patterns around the world.
World Bank Group. (n.d.). El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO): United States. Climate Change Knowledge Portal.
It can bring drought to some agricultural regions and heavy rainfall and flooding to others, threatening harvests and livelihoods. Without early action to protect crops and food supply chains, these weather shocks could drive a significant increase in hunger.
World Bank. (2026). Food security update – October 2026.
The impact of climate-related events on food productivity and availability will likely be uneven across countries and increase instability in the global food supply chain. As a result of food supply chain disruptions, the food insecurity divide between low- and high-income countries may deepen further.
Jägermeyr, J, Müller, C, Ruane, A C, Elliott, J, Balkovic, J, Castillo, O, Faye, B, Foster, I, Folberth, C, Franke, J A et al. 2021. “Climate Impacts on Global Agriculture Emerge Earlier in New Generation of Climate and Crop Models.” Nature 2: 873–885.
Finally, let’s look at the effects that fragility in food supply chains have on hunger.

Fragile food supply chains

In many countries—particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa—hunger has worsened due to conflict and climate shocks. But these pressures are only part of the problem; there is often another reason for continuing hunger: fragile food supply chains. When supply chains are weak, they become the system’s breaking point, and are easily disrupted by violence, extreme weather, or other shocks, amplifying, rather than absorbing, the effects of a crisis.
Kunaka, Charles; Abate, Megersa Abera; Lonla, Théophile Bougna; Molla, Kisanet Haile. 2025. Transport Connectivity for Food Security in Africa: Strengthening Supply Chains. World Bank.
Fragility in food supply chains often stems from gaps in transport infrastructure, storage capacity, and access to markets. In much of Sub-Saharan Africa, food production is enough in aggregate, but poor transport networks prevent food from reaching deficit areas.
Nearly one-third of African countries can produce enough food, but they struggle to supply remote regions.
Food often has to travel extremely long distances, raising the risk of spoilage and waste, or it has to pass through a single critical route, leaving entire regions vulnerable to disruption when these fail.
Kunaka, Charles; Abate, Megersa Abera; Lonla, Théophile Bougna; Molla, Kisanet Haile. 2025. Transport Connectivity for Food Security in Africa: Strengthening Supply Chains. World Bank.
These challenges are clearly reflected in hunger outcomes across Sub-Saharan Africa. Despite some early progress this century, the prevalence of undernourishment remains alarmingly high. Persistently elevated food prices point to deep structural weaknesses.
Kunaka, Charles; Abate, Megersa Abera; Lonla, Théophile Bougna; Molla, Kisanet Haile. 2025. Transport Connectivity for Food Security in Africa: Strengthening Supply Chains. World Bank.
In fact, costs associated with food supply chains can account for nearly half the retail price of staple foods such as rice, grains, and cassava.
More than one-third of locally produced food in Sub-Saharan Africa is lost or wasted as a result of fragile food supply chains, due to inadequate storage and transport infrastructure.
Kunaka, Charles; Abate, Megersa Abera; Lonla, Théophile Bougna; Molla, Kisanet Haile. 2025. Transport Connectivity for Food Security in Africa: Strengthening Supply Chains. World Bank.
With the highest rates of undernourishment, many Sub-Saharan African countries hold the greatest potential for rapid gains. Strengthening food supply chains—by investing in transport, storage, and market access—would reduce losses, stabilize prices, and play a crucial role in improving nutrition and broader health outcomes.
Kunaka, Charles; Abate, Megersa Abera; Lonla, Théophile Bougna; Molla, Kisanet Haile. 2025. Transport Connectivity for Food Security in Africa: Strengthening Supply Chains. World Bank.

A path forward

After decades of progress, global hunger has been rising, driven by conflicts that disrupt livelihoods and markets, climate shocks that damage crops and infrastructure, and fragile supply chains that deepen losses and raise food prices. These pressures reinforce each other, leaving vulnerable communities increasingly exposed to food insecurity.
Yet, this reversal of progress is not inevitable. Countries such as Senegal have beaten the odds, showing that sustained economic growth, stronger agricultural productivity, and coordinated nutrition policies can accelerate progress, even in difficult circumstances.
But turning such gains into broader progress will require urgent, coordinated action to: prevent and respond to food crises; invest in climate-resilient agriculture and reliable transport and storage; strengthen social protection; and improve household-level data so resources reach those who are most at risk.
Maxwell, D, Vaitla, B and Coates, J. 2014. “How Do Indicators of Household Food Insecurity Measure Up? An Empirical Comparison from Ethiopia.” Food Policy 47: 107–116.
With sustained commitment, countries can build food systems that withstand shocks and renew progress toward ending hunger.
