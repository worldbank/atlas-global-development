The Plastic Tide: Tracking Waste From Land To Sea

Plastic is everywhere in modern life, even the ocean. Carried from land to sea, plastic waste harms marine life and coastal livelihoods. As plastic use rises, how can the world stop plastic waste from flowing into the ocean?

December 2026, Story by Ana Florina Pirlea & Daniel Boller, Visuals by Maarten Lambrechts, Illustrations: Mark Watkinson

Plastic production and waste

Plastic is used in virtually all aspects of modern life. It is estimated that, between 1950 and 2019, global use of plastic increased from 2 to 460 million tonnes. And if current trends continue, this is projected to almost triple to over 1.2 billion tonnes by 2060.
OECD (2022), Global Plastics Outlook: Policy Scenarios to 2060, OECD Publishing, Paris
This dramatic rise in plastic use has been matched by an equally dramatic surge in plastic waste. The world is on track to nearly triple its plastic waste by 2060: from over 350 million tonnes to more than 1 billion tonnes. The trash that remains uncollected on the ground and which leaks into rivers and oceans is set to double in volume over the next few decades.
OECD (2022), Global Plastics Outlook: Policy Scenarios to 2060, OECD Publishing, Paris
In 2019 the amount of mismanaged plastic waste was increasing by 2,515 kg/second. In 2060 this is projected to reach 4,839 kg/second (estimates; see ticker in the “Mismanaged” category of the chart).
Plastic can take hundreds of years to degrade. Over long periods of time, large pieces of plastic that end up in the water—such as bottles, bags, and fishing nets—begin to break down due to sunlight, waves, wind, and abrasion,
Allen, S., Allen, D., Karbalaei, S., Maselli, V., & Walker, T. R. (2022). Micro(nano)plastics sources, fate, and effects: What we know after ten years of research. Journal of Hazardous Materials Advances.
slowly decomposing into tiny pieces, called microplastics and nanoplastics. These plastic particles can be found everywhere, from the Antarctic to inside the human body.
United States Environmental Protection Agency. Impact of Plastic Pollution.
Michael Haederle. UNM Researchers Find Alarmingly High Levels of Microplastics in Human Brains – and Concentrations are Growing Over Time. University of New Mexico Health Sciences, February 28, 2025.
Around 1.7 million tonnes of plastic flows into the ocean every year, mainly from rivers, but also directly from coastlines.
OECD (2022), Global Plastics Outlook: Policy Scenarios to 2060, OECD Publishing, Paris
Understanding how plastics reach the ocean is crucial for tackling marine pollution. Large rivers and smaller urban waterways alike carry plastic from land to sea. Plastic waste left on streets, open land, and other surfaces is swept away by rain or melting snow, and carried into nearby streams and rivers. From there, plastics travel downstream to the ocean, especially during periods of heavy rainfall or flooding.
Meijer, Lourens J. J., et al. More than 1000 Rivers Account for 80% of Global Riverine Plastic Emissions into the Ocean. Science Advances, vol. 7, no. 18, 30 Apr. 2021
Plastic waste can also clog drains and exacerbate urban flooding.
Kaza, Silpa; Yao, Lisa C.; Bhada-Tata, Perinaz; Van Woerden, Frank; Levine, Daniel. 2018. What a Waste 2.0: A Global Snapshot of Solid Waste Management to 2050. Urban Development;. © World Bank.
South Asia illustrates this well. It is the world's most populous and densely populated region; it is crossed by numerous transboundary rivers; and less than half of its municipal waste is collected.
Shepardson, Karin; Bhada Tata, Perinaz; Tsydenova, Nina. Waves of Plastic: A Snapshot of Marine Plastic Pollution in South Asia (English). Washington, D.C. : World Bank Group.
Once in the ocean, some plastic waste stays close to the coastlines, while some is moved over long distances by ocean currents and can end up in floating garbage patches. These patches are formed by large rotating ocean systems, called gyres, that draw in and trap debris, especially land‑based plastic, causing it to accumulate at the center of the gyre over time. As debris collects, larger items such as fishing nets and bottles, begin to break down into microplastics, which become even more embedded in the marine environment. Because of the constant movement of currents and winds, the boundaries of these garbage patches shift over time.
Leal Filho, W., Hunt, J., & Kovaleva, M. (2021). Garbage Patches and Their Environmental Implications in a Plastisphere. Journal of Marine Science and Engineering, 9(11), 1289.
The North Pacific Garbage Patch measures approximately 1.6 million square kilometers (about three times the size of France)
The Ocean Cleanup: The Great Pacific Garbage Patch
There are five major ocean gyres—in the North and South Pacific, North and South Atlantic, and Indian Oceans—all of which host significant garbage patches. The largest, the North Pacific Garbage Patch (also called the Great Pacific Garbage Patch), is a massive accumulation of floating plastic debris in the North Pacific Subtropical Gyre, between Hawaii and California. It is not a fixed island of trash, but a dynamic growing accumulation fed by ocean currents and the slow breakdown of plastic already in the ocean.
Lebreton, Laurent, Robin de Vries, Yannick Pham, Helen Wolter, Marjolein van Vulpen, Peter Puskic, Bruno Sainte-Rose, Sarah-Jeanne Royer, and Matthias Egger. 2024. “Seven Years into the North Pacific Garbage Patch: Legacy Plastic Fragments Rising Disproportionally Faster than Larger Floating Objects.” Environmental Research Letters 19: 124054

The impact of ocean plastic pollution

Impact on marine life

Plastic pollution harms marine life in several ways. Many marine animals mistake plastic fragments for food and swallow them. Once ingested, plastic can block their digestive systems, leaving them unable to feed properly and vulnerable to starvation. Plastic may also expose animals to chemicals contained in the material itself or pollutants that accumulate on its surface.
Abahussain AAM, Nasr FA, Jumah AB, Saravanan P, Siva Kumar N, Al-Zharani M, Guganathan L, Sasikumar G, Alsalamah SA, Qurtam AA, Senthilkumar J, Tamizhdurai P. Toxic threats from plastic waste: human health impacts, challenges, and policy solutions. RSC Adv. 2025 Oct 27;15(48):40761-40788.
Pachuau Lalrinfela, Rebecca Vanlalsangi, Khawlhring Lalrinzuali, Punuri Jayasekhar Babu. 2024. Microplastics: Their effects on the environment, human health, and plant ecosystems, Environmental Pollution and Management.
Larger debris, such as discarded fishing nets, which are made almost exclusively of plastics,
United Nations Environment Programme. (2023, June 5). Fishing nets: The double-edged plastic swords in our ocean.
can entangle marine animals, causing injury or death. Together, these impacts threaten marine biodiversity and the ecosystems that support fisheries.
Hahladakis, J. N. (2024). A meta‑research analysis on the biological impact of plastic litter in the marine biota. Science of the Total Environment, 928, 172504.
Plastic ingestion by marine fish is widespread and rising, and has been documented in hundreds of species including many that people eat. There are two reasons for this: first, the amount of plastic in the water continues to increase, and second, the capacity to detect plastic is constantly improving.
Savoca, M. S., McInturf, A. G., & Hazen, E. L. (2021). Plastic ingestion by marine fish is widespread and increasing. Global Change Biology, 27(10), 2188–2199.

In studies published between 2017 and 2019

33 %

of sampled fish contained plastic

Plastic can harm wildlife both directly and by weakening its defenses against other threats. Even small amounts of plastic can kill fish by blocking or puncturing the gut.
E.L. Murphy, B.R. Baechler, L. Roman, G.H. Leonard, N.J. Mallos, R.G. Santos, & C.M. Rochman, A quantitative risk assessment framework for mortality due to macroplastic ingestion in seabirds, marine mammals, and sea turtles, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A. 122 (48) e2415492122, (2025).
Microplastics in the water can also make a viral disease far deadlier. A study found that about 80 percent of trout exposed to nylon microfibers died after infection, versus 20 percent exposed to the virus alone; this is likely because the fibers damaged their gills.
Seeley, M. E., Hale, R. C., Zwollo, P., Vogelbein, W. K., & Wargo, A. R. (2023). Microplastics exacerbate virus‑mediated mortality in fish. Science of the Total Environment, 866, 161191.

Impact on the economy

The costs of marine plastic pollution go beyond environmental damage. Plastic trash in the ocean damages fishing gear, disrupts shipping and transport, and diminishes the appeal of coastal destinations that depend on healthy marine environments. And as plastic production and waste continue to rise, the economic losses could grow into the hundreds of billions of dollars globally by mid-century.
McIlgorm, A., Raubenheimer, K., McIlgorm, D. E., & Nichols, R. (2022). The cost of marine litter damage to the global marine economy: Insights from the Asia-Pacific into prevention and the cost of inaction. Marine Pollution Bulletin, 174, 113167.
These estimates reflect the economic losses, or missed economic opportunities, experienced by industries as a result of marine debris, primarily plastic pollution. They capture the direct impacts on sectors, but do not include the costs of remediation and cleanup, indirect environmental damage, or losses in ecosystem services and natural capital. For example, in the tourism sector, the estimates largely represent reduced visitor spending and income when littered shorelines and beaches become less attractive to tourists. Additional expenses incurred to restore affected areas—such as beach cleanup efforts to attract visitors back— are separate from these losses and not included in the estimates.
McIlgorm, A., Raubenheimer, K., McIlgorm, D. E., & Nichols, R. (2022). The cost of marine litter damage to the global marine economy: Insights from the Asia-Pacific into prevention and the cost of inaction. Marine Pollution Bulletin, 174, 113167.
In Thailand, Malaysia, and the Philippines, less than a quarter of the plastics available for recycling are recovered and turned into useful materials. When single-use plastics are thrown away, more than 75 percent of their material value is lost. Across the three countries, that comes to nearly $6 billion a year.
This mismanaged waste hurts sectors that are central to the region’s economies, including tourism, fisheries and shipping, and affects both livelihoods and infrastructure. To partially recover this lost value and support the move to a circular economy, countries can improve plastic collection and sorting, require new products to contain a share of recycled plastic, make packaging easier to recycle, and build more recycling plants.
Kwakwa, Victoria, and Alfonso García Mora. 2021. “Plastic Waste Is a Growing Menace, and a Wasted Opportunity.” East Asia and Pacific on the Rise (World Bank blog), April 6, 2021
World Bank. 2021. “Better Managing Plastic Waste Could Combat Marine Pollution and Unlock Billions of Dollars for a Circular Economy: Southeast Asia.” Press Release No. 2021/124/EAP, March 22, 2021
The buildup of plastic waste can also destabilize food webs in fisheries and aquaculture, diminishing the quality and availability of products for consumption and trade. Small‑scale fishers, who frequently lack resources and operate in poorly regulated trading environments, are especially exposed to these impacts.
Apete, L., Martin, O. V., & Iacovidou, E. (2024). Fishing plastic waste: Knowns and known unknowns. Marine Pollution Bulletin, 205, Article 116530.
In Viet Nam's Mekong and Red River deltas, marine plastic debris takes a real financial toll on small fishing communities. Direct costs include money spent repairing gear and clearing plastic from nets and propellers, and sorting trash out of their catch. But those visible costs are only part of the story. Opportunity costs include lost fishing time and income when boats are off the water for repair, or catching less due to plastic trash floating in the water.
Nguyen, D., Thanh, P.P., Burdett, H.L. et al. Marine plastic pollution undermines the livelihoods and income of fishing communities in Viet Nam. Commun Earth Environ 7, 452 (2026).

How can countries tackle the problem of plastic waste in the ocean?

Mismanaged plastic waste, which is either managed poorly in dumpsites or uncontrolled facilities or not collected at all, is the primary pathway for plastic leakage. Plastic waste from municipal sources (found in everyday household, commercial, and institutional trash) is far more likely to leak into the environment than plastic waste from non-municipal sources. This is because municipal waste contains many short-lived products, such as single-use packaging, disposable food service items, and personal care products that are quickly discarded.
By contrast, plastics used in vehicles, buildings, and electronic equipment typically remain in use for much longer, delaying their entry into the waste stream.
Globally, almost one third of municipal plastic waste is currently mismanaged, equivalent to about 255,000 tonnes every day, but this varies widely across regions.
Cook, Ed; Ionkova, Kremena; Bhada-Tata, Perinaz; Yadav, Sonakshi; Van Woerden, Frank. 2026. What a Waste 3.0: Global Snapshot of Solid Waste Management Toward Circularity until 2050. Urban Development Series. Washington, DC: World Bank.
Each year, one third of all municipal waste – an estimated 93 million tonnes of waste – is mismanaged, either deposited in uncontrolled facilities or dumpsites or not collected at all.
In Sub-Saharan Africa more than 90 percent of plastic waste is mismanaged and South Asia mismanages about 70 percent of its plastic waste. East Asia & Pacific has a relatively low mismanagement rate (under 20 percent), but because it generates so much plastic, its mismanaged volume remains close to that of Sub-Saharan Africa.
Cook, Ed; Ionkova, Kremena; Bhada-Tata, Perinaz; Yadav, Sonakshi; Van Woerden, Frank. 2026. What a Waste 3.0: Global Snapshot of Solid Waste Management Toward Circularity until 2050. Urban Development Series. Washington, DC: World Bank.
One of the most effective ways of preventing plastic from reaching the ocean is strengthening municipal waste systems, particularly universal waste collection.
Jambeck, J. R., Geyer, R., Wilcox, C., Siegler, T. R., Perryman, M., Andrady, A., … Law, K. L. (2015). Plastic waste inputs from land into the ocean. Science, 347(6223), 768–771.
An integrated approach to reducing plastic leakage should also combine several mutually reinforcing initiatives. First, it should put the basics in place: sustainable financing, a supportive enabling environment, and the institutional capacity needed to collect, sort, and treat plastic waste. Second, it should cut the use of problematic and unnecessary single-use plastics and packaging. Third, it should promote circular product and packaging design, along with reuse and greater material efficiency. Fourth, it should strengthen systems for separating, recovering, and recycling waste. Finally, it should help build viable markets and business models for secondary materials and circular alternatives, so that recovered plastics and substitutes become economically attractive.
The appropriate mix of measures must be tailored to each country’s context, reflecting differences in waste management capacity, policy and institutional frameworks, markets, and plastic leakage pathways.
World Bank. 2022. Where Is the Value in the Chain? Pathways out of Plastic Pollution. Washington, DC: World Bank.
World Bank. 2024. Scaling Innovations for Plastic Circularity with Investment in ASEAN. Washington, DC: World Bank.
Additional measures include interception technologies, which, when strategically placed at leakage hotspots, can catch plastics at key leakage points (streets, drains, rivers, and so on) before they reach the ocean.
Silva, J., Morse, M., & Tamayo-Cañadas, V. (2021). Plastic Waste Capture in Rivers: An Inventory of Current Technologies. Benioff Ocean Initiative, University of California, Santa Barbara.
These measures are also consistent with the achievement of SDG target 14.1 which seeks to prevent and significantly reduce marine pollution of all kinds.
United Nations Statistics Division (UNSD). 2026. Global Indicator Framework for the Sustainable Development Goals and Targets of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. New York: United Nations.
Without stronger action, plastic pollution in the ocean will continue to grow, putting marine ecosystems and the people who depend on them at increasing risk. Turning the tide will require action across the plastic lifecycle, from reducing unnecessary plastic use to improving waste collection, recycling, and stopping leakage before it reaches the sea.
